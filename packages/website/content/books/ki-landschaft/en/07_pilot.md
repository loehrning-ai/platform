# Your first pilot

At a former employer, a group with well over 10,000 employees, the answer to the question "Where is our data?" was: in dozens of Excel files on network drives. That was something to work with. A pilot begins with knowing where the material lies that you want to use for it, and with writing down what it costs you in time today.

## One process, one measurement

Pick a single process and compare it before and after with your own numbers. A first pilot needs nothing more.

1. Choose a process that repeats, has a clear owner and has a result you can check. Do not start with one that decides about people.
2. Measure first, for two weeks: the person who does the process notes start and end and counts how often the result has to be corrected.
3. Decide how you will recognise success, and write the number down before you begin.
4. Let the tool work under supervision for four weeks, with one person who checks every result and counts every error.
5. Compare with the earlier measurement and decide: carry on, change or stop.

A negative result is a valid result. Even a pilot that shows the tool saves no time was worth it if you measured it and did not just feel it. During this time, put no customer data into a tool whose contract you have not read.

Read the result without wishful thinking. If the tool saves time but the corrections increase, the difference counts and not the first number, and if it saves nothing, ask yourself whether the process was the wrong one or the input. And if it works, write down under which conditions it worked, which inputs were needed and who checked the results, so that the next pilot does not start from zero.

## The calculation for it

The calculator below takes three numbers only you know: the hours per week the tool saves you, the value of an hour and the cost of the tool per month. It multiplies and subtracts. It promises no return. It only shows from how many saved hours per week the tool pays for itself at the price you entered. You may count only time that you spend differently.

An invented example makes this concrete: four hours per week at 50 euros give, at 4.33 weeks per month, about 866 euros of time value, and if the tool costs 40 euros a month, 826 euros remain. That is arithmetic and not a result, because nobody guarantees that the four hours become free.

A comparison with others does not tell you what to do. It only helps you judge whether your pilot takes place in an environment where others already talk about it, or in one where hardly anyone does.
