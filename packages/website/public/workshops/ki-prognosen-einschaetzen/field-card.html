The forecaster's field card

Report a forecast as a range and judge it by the decision it changes. This page sums up the 90-minute kit and prints on one A4 sheet.

2The newsvendor rule

Every stocking call weighs two costs:

Cu = cost of being short one unit = lost margin (+ goodwill).
Co = cost of one leftover = unit cost minus salvage.

service level = Cu / (Cu + Co)

When a stockout costs more than a leftover (Cu > Co), the ratio is above 50%, so you order more than the average.

Bakery example: Cu≈4.1, Co≈1.3, CR≈0.76. Croissants spoil overnight, yet a lost sale costs about 3× a leftover. Bake past the average, to about 76% service.

3Safety stock in one line

safety stock = z · σ · √(lead time)

A bigger σ (jumpier demand) needs a bigger cushion. A longer lead time leaves more room for surprises, and the cushion grows with its square root. z is the service level you ask for.

Risk pooling: combine independent demands and σ grows only as √(σ₁²+σ₂²). One shared buffer needs less stock than many separate ones: a central warehouse against many local ones, or a phone and its earbuds planned together.

4Over- or under-predict?

Let the cost ratio decide.

Lean up (order more) when margins are fat, a stockout is costly, goods keep or resell, or resupply is slow.
Lean down (order less) when margins are thin, goods spoil or date quickly, restocking is cheap and fast, or storage is costly.

Worked anchors: gadgets CR≈0.84, bakery CR≈0.76. Both over-order. In both, an empty shelf costs about twice as much as overstock, which is why the ratio usually points up.

5Reading a forecast

MAEHow large is the average miss?
BiasThe average signed error. Near-zero bias with a large MAE means the misses cancel out while every order is still off.
RMSESquares the errors, so a few big misses dominate.
MAPEExplodes near zero demand and treats over and under unevenly.

No value is "good" in general. Compare against your own naive baseline (forecast value added).

A 90% band should hold about 90% of the real points, and it widens with the horizon. A single number hides that uncertainty; the band is what you plan against.

6What a model cannot predict

More data sharpens the probable. It does not help with the genuinely new:

1 Novelty: no precedent, nothing to extrapolate.
2 Regime change: the rules behind the data shift, and history expires.
3 Reflexivity: acting on the forecast changes what is forecast.
4 Singular choice: a one-off event has no distribution to average.

Google Flu Trends overestimated flu levels in 100 of 108 weeks (Lazer et al., Science, 2014). Weather forecasts lose most of their skill after about 10 days.

Five checks before you trust a forecast

  1. Compare against "same as last week". If the model does not beat it, stop.
  2. Pick the model by its backtest score.
  3. Report a range with every forecast.
  4. Test on weeks the model has not seen, from several start dates.
  5. Write down which events the forecast cannot cover.

In the M5 accuracy competition (Walmart sales data, 2020), about 7.5% of 5,507 teams beat plain exponential smoothing. The winner, 22.4% more accurate, combined 220 gradient-boosted tree models and no deep learning. Total demand is predictable; one item in one store is mostly noise.

Sources: M5 competition results (Makridakis et al., 2022) and Lazer et al. (2014). Newsvendor numbers come from a worked bakery example. Prints on one A4 page.
