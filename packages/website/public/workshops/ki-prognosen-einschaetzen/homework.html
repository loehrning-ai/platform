Workshop 01 · Forecasting decisions · Take-home

Forecasting practice

Three levels, from 15 minutes to open-ended. Level 1 uses the hands-on lab, level 2 a spreadsheet and the practice dataset, level 3 your own work. Stop after any level.

Hands-on lab Business case Field card

The five pillars you practise

PillarThe questionWhere it hurts
1 OverpredictWhat does too much cost?Cash frozen, spoilage, markdowns
2 UnderpredictWhat does too little cost?Stockouts, customers who walk away

Three levels

Level 1: set a buffer in the lab

15 minutes

Open the hands-on lab. In act 1 (parcel network) switch the forecast between History only, + Weekday and + Promo dates. In act 2 (device maker) move the Service target slider and compare Local buffers with Shared forecast. The gap between forecast and order is your safety stock.

  1. Run a high service level. Set act 2 to a service target of at least 90%. Watch the cash tied up in inventory grow as stockouts get rare.
  2. Beat the manual rule once. In act 1, find the forecast that beats “same weekday last week” on the capacity bill. Note the one shock no forecast catches.
  3. Write down the buffer that worked. Record the service target that balanced cost and fill rate. Then decide which business needs the bigger buffer: a bakery, where croissants spoil overnight, or a parcel network, where a missed delivery costs more than an idle van. Work it out with the critical ratio.

A 100% service level loses money. Set the buffer from the cost of too much against the cost of too little.

Practises pillars 1 to 3. Field card sections 2 (newsvendor) and 3 (safety stock).

Level 2: score two forecasts

45 minutes

Build two forecasts on the practice dataset and score them. A spreadsheet is enough; a notebook works too.

Dataset: demand-weekly.csv, 104 weeks of one product's demand (week_start, units), with a trend and a bump in Q4. Hide the last 14 rows as your test set, or use your own series.

  1. Split. Hide the last 14 weeks (your test period) so the model never sees the answers. This is backtesting.
  2. Naive baseline. Forecast each test week as last week's actual. This is the bar to beat.
  3. Smoothed forecast. Use a moving average of the last 3 weeks, or exponential smoothing F = 0.3·actual + 0.7·F_prev. In a notebook: SimpleExpSmoothing or Holt.
  4. Score both over the 14 weeks: MAE = AVG(ABS(actual - forecast)); Bias = AVG(forecast - actual) (positive means over-forecast).
  5. Report four numbers (MAE and bias for naive and smoothed) and one sentence: is the smoothed forecast worth the extra step?

Practises baselines and backtesting. Field card section 5 and the five checks.

Level 3: your own work

Open-ended

Pick a business, team or system where something runs out or piles up. Apply the newsvendor rule in five sentences:

  1. Name the domain and the one quantity you would forecast: staff hours, inventory, server capacity, hospital beds or ad budget.
  2. Where does over-forecasting cost you most?
  3. Where does under-forecasting cost you most?
  4. Which is worse, and does your critical ratio point to a high or a low service level?
  5. What buffer would you set, and which signal (a season, a lead time, a promotion) would make you change it?

Practises all five pillars. Field card sections 1 to 4.
