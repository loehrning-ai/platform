Workshop 01 · Forecasting decisions · Take-home

Forecasting practice

Three levels: 15 minutes in the lab, 45 minutes in a spreadsheet, then your own work. Stop after any level.

Three levels

Level 1: set a buffer in the lab

15 minutes

Open the hands-on lab. The gap between forecast and order is your safety stock.

  1. Beat the manual rule. In act 1 (parcel network), switch between History only, + Weekday and + Promo dates until one beats “same weekday last week” on the capacity bill. Note the shock no forecast catches.
  2. Run a high service level. In act 2 (device maker), set the Service target to 90% or more and compare Local buffers with Shared forecast. Cash tied in stock grows as stockouts get rare.
  3. Write down the buffer that worked: the service target that balanced cost and fill rate. Then use the critical ratio to decide who needs the bigger buffer: a bakery, whose croissants spoil overnight, or a parcel network, where a missed delivery costs more than an idle van.

A 100% service level loses money. Set the buffer from the cost of too much against the cost of too little.

Practises pillars 1 to 3. Look up: field card sections 1 to 3.

Level 2: score two forecasts

45 minutes

A spreadsheet is enough; a notebook works too. Dataset: demand-weekly.csv, 104 weeks of one product's demand (week_start, units) with a trend and a bump in Q4, or your own series.

  1. Split. Hide the last 14 weeks as your test period so the model never sees the answers (backtesting).
  2. Naive baseline. Forecast each test week as last week's actual. This is the bar to beat.
  3. Smoothed forecast. A 3-week moving average, or exponential smoothing F = 0.3·actual + 0.7·F_prev. In a notebook: SimpleExpSmoothing or Holt.
  4. Score both over the 14 weeks: MAE = AVG(ABS(actual - forecast)); Bias = AVG(forecast - actual) (positive means over-forecast).
  5. Report MAE and bias for both forecasts and one sentence: is smoothing worth the extra step?

Practises baselines and backtesting. Look up: “Reading a forecast” below and the field card checks.

Level 3: your own work

Open-ended

Pick a business, team or system where something runs out or piles up. Apply the newsvendor rule in five sentences:

  1. The domain and the one quantity you would forecast (staff hours, inventory, server capacity, hospital beds, ad budget).
  2. Where over-forecasting costs you most.
  3. Where under-forecasting costs you most.
  4. Which is worse, and whether your critical ratio points to a high or low service level.
  5. The buffer you would set, and which signal (a season, a lead time, a promotion) would change it.

Practises all five pillars. Look up: field card sections 1 to 3.

Reading a forecast

  • MAE: the average miss. Bias: the average signed error; near-zero bias with a large MAE means the misses cancel while every order is off.
  • RMSE squares errors, so a few big misses dominate. MAPE explodes near zero demand and treats over and under unevenly.
  • No value is “good” in general. Compare against your own naive baseline (forecast value added).
  • A 90% band should hold about 90% of real points and widens with the horizon. Plan against the band.
  • Safety stock grows with σ and with the square root of lead time.
  • Risk pooling: combined independent demands grow only as √(σ₁²+σ₂²), so one shared buffer (a central warehouse, a phone and its earbuds planned together) needs less stock than many separate ones.
  • What no model predicts: novelty (no precedent), regime change (the rules behind the data shift), reflexivity (acting on the forecast changes it) and one-off choices (no distribution). Google Flu Trends overestimated flu in 100 of 108 weeks; weather forecasts lose most skill after about 10 days.
  • M5 competition (Walmart sales, 2020): about 7.5% of 5,507 teams beat plain exponential smoothing. The winner, 22.4% more accurate, combined 220 gradient-boosted tree models and no deep learning. Total demand is predictable; one item in one store is mostly noise.

Sources: Makridakis et al. (2022), Lazer et al., Science (2014). Newsvendor numbers come from a worked bakery example.
